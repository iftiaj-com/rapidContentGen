# Craft: character, scene, dialogue, suspense, setups

The principles below are widely taught in screenwriting. Each one names where it comes from, so the
agent can tell a well-known source from a rule of thumb. Sources and what was checked: `sources.md`.
None of this was measured by this project. Treat the rules as defaults that a strong reason can break.

## 1. Drama is a person under pressure

**Want.** The protagonist wants something concrete that a camera can see: an object, a place, a
person, an outcome. "Happiness" is not a want; "get to the station before the last train" is.

**Need.** What the protagonist lacks inside (trust, courage, honesty). The want makes them face it. In
the best endings, getting the want and meeting the need pull against each other, and the choice between
them is the climax. Under about 3 minutes, a want alone is enough.

**Obstacle.** Another person, a rule, nature, time, or the protagonist's own flaw. The story is only as
strong as the obstacle. If the want could be reached in one easy step, there is no film.

**Stakes.** What is lost on failure, made personal. "The company closes" is weak; "she has to tell her
father she lost his shop" is strong.

**Mamet's three questions** (from a memo to the writers of the TV series *The Unit*, widely reposted
since 2010; the exact wording varies between copies). Ask them of every scene:
1. Who wants what?
2. What happens if they don't get it?
3. Why now?
The same memo puts the job of making each scene dramatic on the writer, not the actors or director,
and calls any scene where characters explain things to each other a failure. Use the idea; do not quote
the memo on screen or to the user as exact words.

**Character is choice under pressure.** We learn who someone is from what they do when it costs them
something, not from what they say about themselves or what others say about them. Give the protagonist
at least one choice where both options cost something.

**Active, not passive.** The protagonist drives the climax by a decision. Things that happen *to* them
can start the story; things they *do* must end it. A coincidence that helps the hero at the end reads as
cheating. (The same point appears in the Pixar storytelling list Emma Coats tweeted in 2011:
coincidences that get characters into trouble are fine, ones that get them out are not.)

**A trait we can see.** Give each character one specific behavior (taps the counter, folds receipts,
never sits down). It saves dialogue and makes them real in two seconds of screen time.

## 2. Every scene turns

**The value turn** (Robert McKee, *Story*, 1997, and his site's "Do your scenes turn?"). Name the
value at stake in the scene (hope, trust, safety, love, freedom) and its charge, positive or negative,
at the start. At the end, the charge must have changed. If it has not, nothing happened: the scene is a
"non-event". Cut it or make it turn. The change should come from what a character does.

**Scene shape.** Each scene is a small story: a want, a tactic, resistance, a new tactic, and a turn.
Enter as late as possible (skip the hellos and the walk to the door) and leave as soon as the turn
lands. This is common screenwriting advice; it is often attributed to particular writers, but no single
source is given here.

**Gap between expectation and result.** A character acts, expecting one result, and gets another. That
gap forces a new action. It is the engine under the turn. (This framing is usually linked to McKee; the
exact phrase was not confirmed in the book, see `sources.md`.)

**"But" and "therefore", not "and then".** Between scenes, the next one happens *because of* the
last (therefore) or *in spite of* it (but). "And then" means a list of events, not a story. Widely
credited to Trey Parker and Matt Stone (the South Park creators) from a talk to a film class; the
attribution is general, not a quote.

**Escalation.** Each turn costs more than the last. The obstacle gets stronger, the time shorter, the
choice harder. Under 3 minutes, two turns plus the climax is often all there is room for.

## 3. Suspense, surprise and what the audience knows

In the Truffaut interviews (recorded 1962, published as *Hitchcock/Truffaut*, 1966-67 and later
editions), Hitchcock gives the bomb-under-the-table example: if a bomb goes off without warning, the
audience gets a few seconds of surprise; if they see the bomb and the clock first, the same ordinary
conversation becomes minutes of suspense. He adds an exception: surprise is right when the twist
itself is the point. Secondary copies of the passage differ in wording, so paraphrase it.

Use it in planning:
- **Suspense:** tell the audience the danger (a clock, a letter in the wrong bag, a car the character
  has not seen). Then hold. Tension lasts as long as the scene.
- **Surprise:** keep it from everyone. It lasts a moment. Use it once, for the twist, and plant it so a
  second viewing finds the clue.
- **Dramatic irony:** the audience knows what a character does not. It turns small talk into tension.
- **Mystery:** the character knows something the audience does not. It makes us lean in, but too long
  and we stop caring.

Write the "audience knows" column of each scene card. If the audience never knows more or less than
the protagonist, the film is probably flat.

## 4. Show, don't tell (subtext)

- Action lines describe only what a camera and a microphone can record. No "she feels betrayed".
  Write what she does: "She puts the ring on the counter and does not look at it."
- **Subtext.** People rarely say what they want. Dialogue is a tactic to get the want: they flatter,
  dodge, joke, threaten. The real meaning sits under the words. If a line states the feeling directly,
  rewrite it as a behavior or as a line about something else.
- **No "as you know, Bob".** Two characters never tell each other what both already know, to inform
  the audience. Let the information come out in conflict, or show it.
- **Exposition as ammunition.** Give facts only when a character needs them to win the scene, and as
  late as possible.
- **Short lines.** A short film has no room for speeches. Most lines are under about ten words.
  Interruptions and silences say more than complete sentences.
- **Silence is a line.** A held look, no answer, a door closing. Mark them in the script.
- **One voice each.** Cover the names and read the dialogue: if you cannot tell who speaks, the
  characters are not distinct.

## 5. Setups and payoffs

A setup is a thing shown early (an object, a skill, a line, a rule) that matters later. A payoff is
the moment it matters. The idea is often called "Chekhov's gun", after advice in Anton Chekhov's letters
that is quoted in many forms; no exact wording is given here.

- Every important payoff needs a setup, planted while the audience is looking elsewhere. Then the
  twist feels inevitable, not convenient.
- Every visible setup needs a payoff. A close-up of an object is a promise.
- The strongest setups do double duty: the red stamp shows Mara's habit in scene 1 and decides the
  ending in scene 4.
- **Callbacks:** a line, gesture or image from the opening returns at the end with a new meaning. It
  is the cheapest way to make a short feel complete.

## 6. Theme and the ending

- **Controlling idea** (McKee's term): one sentence that names the value at the end and the cause
  ("Honesty wins back trust when it costs something"). The writer knows it; the film never says it.
- **Write the ending first.** Then work back so every scene leads to it. A strong ending is
  surprising and inevitable at once: unexpected in the moment, obvious looking back.
- **Answer the dramatic question.** Yes, no, or yes-but (they get the want and lose something), or
  no-but (they fail and gain the need). An open ending is a choice; even then, the protagonist must
  have changed or chosen.
- **The final image** mirrors the first: the same place changed, the same action done differently,
  the empty chair now filled. Plan the two images together.

## 7. Short films specifically

From the short-film writing literature (for example Pat Cooper and Ken Dancyger, *Writing the Short
Film*) and common festival practice, summarized here as defaults:

- One main character, one situation, one turn that matters. A short is not a compressed feature.
- Few locations (one to three), few speaking characters (one to three), a short span of story time.
  Each extra location or character costs screen time for setup.
- Start as close to the turn as possible. The setup of a feature (act one) shrinks to seconds: a single
  image or action that shows who they are and what they want.
- Visual storytelling carries more weight than in a feature, because there is no time for dialogue to
  build character. Find the image that says it.
- The ending is the most remembered part. Spend the most rewriting time on it and on the first image.

## 8. Writing for an editor that works on footage

In rcg, many "films" are built from footage that already exists. The craft still applies, with two
changes:

- **Find the story in the footage.** Measure the shots (`cine.mjs measure`), then ask: who is the
  protagonist the footage can support? What does the footage show them wanting, trying or reaching?
  Order shots by cause and effect, not by when they were filmed.
- **The Kuleshov principle.** A shot's meaning depends on the shot next to it: a neutral face reads as
  hunger, grief or desire by what is cut against it. The original experiment's footage is lost and the
  accounts differ (`sources.md`), but the editing principle is standard and is how a story is built from
  footage that was not shot as a story. Use it honestly: never cut real people so that they appear to
  react to something they did not see, in a way that changes what happened.
- **Narration as the missing scene.** When the footage has no conflict, a voiceover can carry the want
  and the obstacle (a letter, a memory, a promise). It must add what the picture cannot, never describe
  it.
